Impulse versus intention

Every time we reach for a phone or an AI tool, we can ask, is this impulse or intention?

Technology is here, and it's for everyone. If we don't use it, we fall behind.

However, we shouldn't let it replace our own 1judgment, our 2critical thinking, our 3curiosity.

When many of us were growing up in the 1990s, we knew a dozen phone numbers by heart and did our sums in our heads. Then the calculator took the sums, the mobile phone took the numbers, and maps took our sense of direction. Each time, we gave up something small and grew a lot.

Gen Z grew up with a smartphone in hand. Today's children are growing up with AI. This revolution is different.

For the first time, the tool can do our thinking and reasoning.

So think first, then ask. Leading AI, not the other way round.

Be the watcher

When you think of yourself as a watcher, life is like a movie. In this movie, technology is one character, a big one in this chapter. Use it as much as it helps you, and watch yourself using it.

In a theater, you can get so absorbed that you feel you are living in the story. Yet you are still in your seat, watching. You are that watcher. Even deep in your phone or an AI tool, you can stay in your seat and not be consumed by it.

This watcher is the witness, sakshi in Sanskrit. It is a concept in Advaita Vedanta, a school of Indian philosophy, and it runs through the Ashtavakra Gita, one of the sacred scriptures of Hinduism. It is the idea I base my life on.

Often your hand goes to the phone before you have decided anything. The watcher sees that. Watching doesn't mean stopping. It means you respond to the impulse rather than just react.

Before blaming the buzz, notice your reach.

Think First, Then Ask

How to use AI and technology to their full potential without outsourcing your thinking, attention or judgment.

The risk is simple. If you ask AI first, you may begin with its framing of the problem, not your own.

And over time that can quietly weaken curiosity, independent reasoning and judgment. The talk draws on studies of when to bring AI into our thinking and of what starts most phone pickups. It says plainly what they do and don't show. And it brings in the witness, an old practice that helps us watch ourselves while we use AI.

About

I grew up in a small town in northern India, moved to the US in 2017, and now live in Idaho. About two decades of my life have gone into studying how fast technology is changing. A student of the Indian philosophies of Vedanta, Advaita and Kashmir Shaivism, and a big believer in technology, I carry both equally. And I'm a parent of two teenagers in this fast-moving world of AI and social media. I have to get this right, so I can help them become better humans. That is why this work matters so much to me.
